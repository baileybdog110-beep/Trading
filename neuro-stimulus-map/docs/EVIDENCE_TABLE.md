## Grading rubric

| Category | Meaning |
|---|---|
| strong | Consistent findings summarised by at least one meta-analysis, a review of many studies, or a large-sample study (n of about 100 or more), AND at least one further independent supporting source; the studied process closely matches the process named here. A strong general association can still apply only weakly to a particular clip. |
| moderate | Consistent findings from at least two human studies or one meta-analysis, but with small samples, a single research program, or notable indirectness. |
| limited | A single study, few small studies, or indirect evidence. Plausible but not established. |
| contested | Credible studies disagree about the association. Shown so the disagreement is visible, not as an established finding. |
| insufficient | No verified record in this evidence base supports a mapping. Shown in gray. Gray never means 'inactive' - every region is active all the time. |

| Applicability | Meaning |
|---|---|
| direct | The detected feature is the same kind of stimulus the studies used (e.g., human voices for voice-area studies). |
| partial | The feature is related but differs in important ways (laboratory stimuli vs. naturalistic media, a coarse proxy, or assumed comprehension). |
| extrapolation | Applying the research requires assumptions about the person (what they notice, understand, enjoy or feel) that the media cannot show. |

## Feature → process rules

| Feature | Process | Applicability | Min. detection | Rationale | Assumes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sound present | Basic auditory processing | direct | moderate | Studies of auditory cortex used sounds of all kinds; audible sound is the defining condition. | The listener can hear the audio (it is played at an audible level). |
| Large loudness change | Coding of sound level / loudness | partial | moderate | Level-dependence of auditory cortex responses was shown with controlled sound levels; the file's relative level change is only a proxy for level at the ear. | Playback volume is unknown; the change is relative to the recording. |
| Abrupt sound onsets | Detecting acoustic onsets | partial | moderate | Onset-responsive cortex was characterised with speech; abrupt onsets in media include non-speech sounds. |  |
| Abrupt sound onsets | Involuntary orienting to salient change | extrapolation | moderate | Oddball and sensory-change studies used unexpected deviants in simple sequences. Whether a sudden sound in media captures attention depends on expectation, attention and context. | The event is unexpected for the listener and not ignored. |
| Speech present | Voice perception | direct | low | Voice-area studies used speech and non-speech vocal sounds, which is what this feature detects. |  |
| Spoken words (transcript) | Sentence-level language comprehension | partial | moderate | Language-network studies used sentences the participants understood; transcribed words show that language is present, not that it is understood. | The listener understands the language being spoken. The listener is attending to the speech. |
| Spoken words (transcript) | Semantic processing (word and concept meaning) | partial | moderate | Meaningful words engage semantic processing in listeners who understand them; naturalistic podcast stories (Huth et al. 2016) are a close match for spoken content. | The listener understands the language being spoken. |
| Narrative / storytelling cues | Integrating a narrative over time | extrapolation | moderate | Naturalistic story studies show long-timescale integration in higher-order areas when a story is coherent and followed; lexical cues only suggest a story is being told. | The listener follows the story over time. Interpretation depends on the listener's beliefs (Yeshurun et al. 2017). |
| Mental-state language | Thinking about others' mental states (theory of mind) | extrapolation | moderate | Theory-of-mind studies asked participants to reason about beliefs and intentions; mental-state words are only a cue that such reasoning may be invited. | The listener engages with what speakers or characters think or want. |
| Music present | Music-selective auditory processing | direct | low | Music-selective components were identified with natural music clips; detected music is the same stimulus class. |  |
| Music present | Music-evoked pleasure and emotion (listener-dependent) | extrapolation | moderate | Meta-analyses pooled studies where listeners experienced, or were expected to experience, pleasure or emotion. The music alone cannot establish that a listener does. | The listener finds this music pleasurable or emotionally moving. Familiarity strongly changes responses (Pereira et al. 2011). |
| Music present | Musical structure processing in language regions (contested) | partial | moderate | Shown to illustrate a genuine scientific disagreement about whether music structure is processed in language regions. |  |
| Possible singing / vocals with music | Song perception | partial | low | Song-selective responses were identified for sung music; this detection is low-confidence and may reflect speech over music. |  |
| Regular beat | Beat and rhythm perception | partial | moderate | Beat studies mostly used controlled rhythms; a naturalistic replication found rhythm-feature responses less reliable than timbral ones (Burunat et al. 2016). | The listener perceives the beat. |
| Visual content | Early and intermediate visual processing | direct | moderate | Any viewed image engages occipital visual cortex; movie studies confirm this for film. | The viewer is looking at the screen. |
| Large brightness change | Early visual response to non-motion image change | partial | moderate | In natural movies, early visual areas tracked non-motion image changes such as illumination (single study). | The viewer is looking at the screen. |
| Visual motion | Visual motion processing | partial | moderate | Motion-sensitive cortex responds to motion in natural movies; this app's motion estimate is a coarse proxy. | The viewer is looking at the screen. |
| Faces visible | Face perception | direct | moderate | Face-selective fusiform cortex responded to faces within an unconstrained feature film (Hasson et al. 2004) as well as to face photographs. | The viewer is looking at the screen. |
| Places / spatial layout prominent | Scene and place perception | partial | high + confirmation | Scene-selective regions respond to spatial layout in scene images; the feature is user-marked. | The viewer is looking at the screen. |
| Scene cut / shot change | Event segmentation | extrapolation | moderate | Cuts often coincide with situation changes, but when those are controlled, cuts per se were not positively associated with perceived event boundaries (Magliano & Zacks 2011). | The cut marks a change of situation or action for the viewer. |
| Situation change (new time, place or action) | Event segmentation | direct | high + confirmation | Event-boundary studies used moments that observers judged to be transitions between events, which is what this user annotation marks. | Other viewers may place boundaries differently. |
| Humor (interpretation) | Humor comprehension (getting the joke) | extrapolation | high + confirmation | Humor studies time-locked responses to moments participants found humorous; a marked joke may not be understood or found funny by a given listener. | The listener understands the joke. |
| Humor (interpretation) | Humor appreciation (mirth) | extrapolation | high + confirmation | Appreciation responses were tied to moments participants enjoyed. | The listener finds it funny. |
| Suspense (interpretation) | Suspense-related attentional engagement | extrapolation | high + confirmation | Two naturalistic film studies related suspense ratings (from other viewers) to frontal and parietal activity. | The viewer is invested in the story and experiences suspense. |

| Emotional theme (interpretation) | Specific emotion categories (e.g., fear, sadness, joy) | extrapolation | high + confirmation | Kept so that a marked emotional theme produces an explicit 'insufficient evidence' result. |  |

## Deliberately not mapped (insufficient evidence)

| Topic | Reason |
|---|---|
| Specific emotions (fear, sadness, joy, anger...) mapped to specific regions | A meta-analysis comparing 'one emotion, one region' accounts with network accounts found that discrete emotion categories do not consistently and specifically correspond to distinct regions (for example, fear is not always accompanied by amygdala activity). Emotional content in media also does not tell us what a viewer feels. We therefore show no region-specific map for emotional themes. |
| 'Fear centre' / 'pleasure centre' labels | Regions such as the amygdala and nucleus accumbens take part in many processes (relevance, learning, salience, reward of many kinds) and work within distributed networks. Single-function labels are not supported. |
| Dopamine release, hormone levels, or neurotransmitter estimates | Dopamine release has been measured with PET in specific listeners hearing music they chose (Salimpoor et al. 2011). Neurochemistry cannot be inferred from a media file, so the app never displays it. |
| Activation percentages, firing rates or 'how intensely your brain fires' | fMRI measures slow blood-oxygenation changes that follow neural activity indirectly; published maps summarise groups of people under particular conditions. Nothing here measures anyone's brain, so no intensity values are shown - colours are evidence categories. |
| Specific tempo values (e.g., 90 vs 128 BPM) mapped to regions | The curated evidence supports an association for perceiving a regular beat, not for particular tempo values. The estimated tempo is shown as a detected feature only. |
| Narrative surprise or plot twists mapped to regions | Oddball studies concern unexpected sensory events in simple sequences; no verified record in this evidence base links narrative surprise in media to specific regions. Sudden sounds are handled separately (abrupt onsets). |
| Loud or sudden sounds mapped to fear or emotional arousal regions | Level changes and onsets are mapped to auditory and attentional processes that the evidence supports; the curated evidence does not justify inferring emotional arousal or fear from sound level. |
| Neurosynth / NeuroQuery maps as predictions for your media | These tools summarise where the literature reports findings for a term. Coordinate extraction is noisy, terms are lexical rather than validated cognitive states, and maps are not validated predictions for arbitrary stimuli. They informed curation only and are not used to colour the brain. |
| Recognising who is on screen, or reading their facial expressions | The app only detects whether a face is present. It does not identify people or classify expressions, and facial expressions do not reveal what a viewer feels. |
